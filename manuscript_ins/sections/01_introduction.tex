\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Graph neural networks (GNNs)~\citep{kipf2016semi,velickovic2017graph} are used for credit scoring, bail decisions and social recommendation, settings in which the data are split across institutions and the predictions affect people. Federated learning~\citep{mcmahan2017communication} lets such institutions train one model without pooling their graphs. Three requirements then arise together: the model should not discriminate between demographic groups~\citep{dai2021say,agarwal2021towards}; the federation should not learn more about any client, or about any person's sensitive attribute, than it needs to~\citep{abadi2016deep,mcmahan2018learning}; and a few compromised clients should not be able to steer the model~\citep{blanchard2017krum,yin2018byzantine,baruch2019little}.

The mechanisms that address these requirements do not compose. Fair federated methods reweight clients or calibrate the model using per-client fairness or loss reports~\citep{ezzeldin2023fairfed,li2020fair,fedfact2025}, which the server must read and a Byzantine client can forge. Byzantine-robust aggregation rules such as the coordinate median, the trimmed mean and Krum~\citep{yin2018byzantine,blanchard2017krum} need individual updates and therefore cannot run under secure aggregation~\citep{bonawitz2017practical}, the standard tool for hiding individual updates from the server. Private fair methods~\citep{corbucci2024puffle,ling2024feddp} add noise to gradients but assume honest clients. A further threat falls between these lines of work: a minority of clients can \emph{poison fairness}, training their local models to widen the gap between groups while still fitting the labels. Such updates are not outliers, so robust aggregation does not filter them, and a protocol that does not monitor fairness does not notice them.

This paper asks what a federation can achieve if its server only ever sees \emph{sums over clients}, each of whose summands is \emph{range-checked} so that it can be computed under verifiable secure aggregation~\citep{corrigangibbs2017prio,lycklama2023rofl}. We show that this restriction is enough to enforce a global group-fairness budget on graphs of moderate size, to privatise the information the budget needs, and to limit fairness poisoning through updates. Our experiments simulate the cryptographic layer in plaintext; we do not implement or benchmark it. Our protocol, \clamp{}, has three components:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Clipped secure aggregation of updates.} Clients clip their updates to norm $C$, and a validity check rejects any update above the bound; the server sees only the sum.
\item \textbf{Private group statistics.} Clients report, per group, the sum of predictions and the count of validation nodes, computed with a forward pass that never reads the sensitive attribute. The statistics have constant sensitivity and are summed with Gaussian noise.
\item \textbf{One global Lagrange multiplier.} From the noisy sums the server estimates the global demographic-parity gap, updates a single multiplier by projected dual ascent, and broadcasts it with the sign of the gap; clients add a signed penalty to their local loss. The server returns the latest model whose released gap met the budget.
\end{itemize}
There are no per-client weights, scores or reputations, and every server-side computation runs on sums.

\paragraph{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item We formulate fairness-constrained federated graph learning under a server that sees only validated sums and an adversary that controls a minority of clients' updates and fairness statistics (Section~\ref{sec:problem}).
\item We propose \clamp{}, which enforces a global demographic-parity budget through a single broadcast multiplier computed from privatised aggregate statistics, together with an output rule that returns a model satisfying the budget on validation data (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
\item We prove attribute-level differential privacy of the statistics channel, a per-round bound on the influence of Byzantine updates, a bound on the influence of falsified statistics, and a fairness guarantee for the returned model, each with its scope stated (Section~\ref{sec:guarantees}).
\item We evaluate \clamp{} on eight benchmarks against eleven baselines with ten seeds and paired significance tests (Sections~\ref{sec:setup}--\ref{sec:results}). On the three larger graphs whose unconstrained gap exceeds the budget, \clamp{} lowers the soft demographic-parity gap of unconstrained clipped training by 38--75\%, to a mean at or below the budget (in 5 to 9 of 10 seeds), at an AUC within 0.01; the budget sets the operating point on Pokec-z and, down to about 0.012, on Credit. In a three-seed attack study, clipping on sums is comparable to coordinate-wise rules that need individual updates on most update attacks, and under fairness poisoning on Pokec-z \clamp{} keeps the gap at 0.037--0.049 while FedAvg, the median, the trimmed mean and clipping alone reach 0.11--0.20. Baselines that read client reports in the clear reach lower gaps. Control is weak on the three small graphs, and a single client that falsifies its statistics can disable it (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{enumerate}
