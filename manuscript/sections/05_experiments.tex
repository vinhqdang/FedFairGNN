\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\paragraph{Datasets.}
We use five real datasets with \emph{genuine} sensitive attributes
(Table~\ref{tab:datasets}): \textbf{German} (credit; gender), \textbf{Credit}
(default; age), and \textbf{Bail} (recidivism; race)---the standard fair-GNN
benchmarks used by our fairness baselines; \textbf{Pokec} (social; region); and
\textbf{Elliptic} (Bitcoin AML; $203{,}769$ transactions,
$234{,}355$ edges), a large-scale \emph{crypto} graph on which we are, to our
knowledge, the first to study group fairness, using an operational early/late
temporal subgroup as a documented proxy. Data are partitioned across clients by
a Dirichlet($\alpha{=}0.5$) label split with a small floor to avoid degenerate
silos.

\subsection{Baselines and their reimplementation}
\label{sec:baselines}
We benchmark against twelve methods chosen to cover every axis of the trust
problem and every competing school of thought, plus our own variants. Because a
fair comparison requires that all methods run in the \emph{same} federated
graph pipeline, we reimplemented each within our framework; we describe each
faithfully and flag any adaptation. All share the 2-layer GNN backbone,
optimiser, and training budget of Section~\ref{sec:setup} unless a method's
definition requires otherwise.

\begin{description}
\item[Non-fair backbones.] \textbf{FedAvg-GCN} and \textbf{FedAvg-GAT}
\cite{kipf2016semi,velickovic2017graph,mcmahan2017communication} are the
standard graph convolutional and attention networks trained with vanilla FedAvg.
They establish the accuracy ceiling and the unmitigated bias floor against which
fairness interventions are measured.

\item[Fair GNNs (run federated).] \textbf{FairGNN} \cite{dai2021say} attaches an
adversary that predicts the sensitive attribute from the node embedding and
trains the encoder to fool it (a gradient-reversal minimax), which we run at each
client with FedAvg aggregation. \textbf{FairSIN} \cite{yang2024fairsin}
neutralises sensitive information by augmenting each node with the mean features
of its \emph{heterogeneous} (different-group) neighbours, falling back to an MLP
estimate where no such neighbour exists; we implement the FairSIN-F variant.
These are the strongest centralised fair-GNN ideas transplanted to the federated
setting, and FairSIN in particular is the closest published analogue to FSER
(feature-space rather than edge-space debiasing).

\item[Fairness-aware FL aggregation.] \textbf{FairFed} \cite{ezzeldin2023fairfed}
starts from sample-size weights and adjusts each client's weight by the gap
between its local fairness metric and the mean gap, down-weighting clients that
worsen global fairness. \textbf{q-FedAvg} \cite{li2020fair} reweights clients by
a power $q$ of their loss to equalise performance across clients (client, not
group, fairness---included to represent that school). \textbf{FedFB} ports
FairBatch-style group reweighting to the local objective. These are aggregation-
or loss-level baselines that rival BFWA directly.

\item[Federated fair GNN.] \textbf{F\textsuperscript{2}GNN} \cite{f2gnn2023}
combines a model-fairness aggregation weight (favouring low-disparity clients)
with a structure-aware group-balance weight, fused by a temperature-scaled
softmax---the most direct federated-graph-fairness competitor.

\item[Differential privacy.] \textbf{DP-FedAvg} \cite{mcmahan2018learning} clips
the full client gradient to norm $C$ and adds isotropic Gaussian noise,
accounted with the same R\'enyi-DP composition we use for FTGD. It is the crucial
control that isolates the value of FTGD's targeted mechanism at matched
$\epsilon$.

\item[2026 competitors.] \textbf{FaVGNN} \cite{wang2026favgnn} is, in the
original, a \emph{vertical}-FL method whose client side performs
completion-driven adversarial fusion; since our setting is horizontal and all
sensitive attributes are observed, we port its two transferable client-side
components---heterogeneous-neighbour feature fusion and adversarial sensitive
debiasing---and label the result a horizontal adaptation. \textbf{FDP-Fair}
\cite{xue2026fdpfair} trains under differential privacy and then calibrates
group-specific decision offsets on held-out data to enforce demographic parity;
we implement its two-step DP-then-post-process recipe. Together these are the
most recent methods occupying our niche.

\item[2025--2026 head-to-head.] Five further recent methods are reimplemented
from their published algorithm descriptions and benchmarked directly (none
release a public federated-graph implementation; Appendix~\ref{app:baselines}
states exactly what each reimplementation keeps and drops). \textbf{FairGFL}
\cite{fairgfl2025} reweights aggregation by an overlap-ratio proxy (here, a
client's sample-count deviation from the mean, since our single-graph
partition has no literal multi-graph overlap). \textbf{FedGraph-Fair}
\cite{fedgraphfair2025} contributes a minimax/distributionally-robust dual
weight that up-weights high-loss clients (we reproduce this core, dropping its
orthogonal personalised-model/graph-mixing layer). \textbf{PUFFLE}
\cite{corbucci2024puffle} replaces a static fairness weight with a momentum
feedback controller that auto-tunes it toward a target demographic-parity
gap, combined with DP-SGD. \textbf{FedFACT} \cite{fedfact2025} generalises
FDP-Fair's single group-offset to a two-level global-\emph{and}-local
correction (a shared offset plus a per-client offset that is never
aggregated); we implement the closed-form special case for binary
demographic parity, an exact reduction of the paper's own optimum for that
case. \textbf{PoPETs'25 FairFed} \cite{bendoukha2025popets} is FairFed's
weighting rule made homomorphically computable by replacing its
$\exp(-\beta|\cdot|)$ term with a degree-2 polynomial; we reproduce this
statistical core (the paper's actual contribution---a threshold-CKKS secure
aggregation protocol---has no effect on the cleartext numeric result and is
not reimplemented).

\item[Ours.] \textbf{TrustFedGNN} (FSER + FTGD-DP + BFWA), \textbf{TrustFedGNN~(no
DP)} (the same without privacy noise, to isolate the fairness mechanism), and
\textbf{TrustFedGNN-Robust} (BFWA replaced by the Byzantine-robust variant).
\end{description}

We additionally reproduce the fairness-poisoning optimisation attack of
\cite{kasyap2025attack} and, in the robustness study, the classical Krum,
Multi-Krum, coordinate-median and trimmed-mean aggregators
\cite{blanchard2017krum,yin2018byzantine} as robustness baselines.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
All methods share a 2-layer GNN ($d_h{=}64$), AdamW ($\eta{=}0.01$), $E{=}2$
local epochs, and dataset-specific rounds. We report mean\,$\pm$\,std over three
seeds on the pooled global test set. Unless stated, DP targets $\epsilon{=}8$,
$\delta{=}10^{-5}$; the fairness budget is $\tau{=}0.05$. Every table and figure
is regenerated from logged runs by \texttt{experiments/report.py}.

\subsection{Main comparison: fairness--utility}
\label{sec:exp-main}
Tables~\ref{tab:main-auc}--\ref{tab:main-eod} report utility (AUC), demographic
parity, and equal-opportunity gaps for all methods on all datasets. Three
patterns recur across datasets and reward a closer reading.

First, the non-fair backbones (FedAvg-GCN/GAT) set the accuracy ceiling but
carry the largest disparities: message passing does propagate group bias exactly
as Section~\ref{sec:fser} argued, and doing nothing about it is not a neutral
choice but the least fair one. Second, the dedicated fairness methods behave as
their design predicts. Aggregation-level methods (FairFed, F\textsuperscript{2}GNN)
and representation-level methods (FairGNN, FairSIN) both reduce $\dpd$ and
$\eod$ appreciably, and on the smallest dataset (German) the best-tuned of them
can match or slightly edge out TrustFedGNN on a \emph{single} fairness metric in
isolation. This is expected and, we argue, beside the point: those methods buy
their fairness while providing \emph{no} privacy guarantee and, as
Section~\ref{sec:exp-robust} shows, while remaining fully exposed to poisoning.
Third, and most importantly, TrustFedGNN delivers a fairness--utility trade-off
that is competitive with the best of these specialised methods \emph{while
simultaneously} carrying an $(\epsilon{=}8)$-DP guarantee for the sensitive
attribute---something none of the fairness baselines offer. The proper reading
of the main tables is therefore not cell-by-cell but holistic: TrustFedGNN is the
only method that is never far from the frontier on \emph{any} axis, which is
precisely what the composite trust score of Section~\ref{sec:trust} formalises.

The five 2025--2026 reimplementations (Table~\ref{tab:main-auc}--\ref{tab:main-eod},
between the F$^2$GNN-family rows and the DP rule) confirm this pattern rather
than overturn it. FairGFL, FedGraph-Fair, and the PoPETs'25 FairFed variant
all land in the same AUC/DPD band as FairFed and F$^2$GNN---sensible, since
all four are variations on aggregation reweighting---while FedFACT's two-level
offset achieves the lowest DPD among non-private methods on Bail ($0.015$,
Table~\ref{tab:main-dpd}), consistent with its provable-calibration design.
PUFFLE is the one outlier worth naming directly: its momentum controller, left
to auto-tune against the target disparity we configured, drives $\lambda$
aggressively toward $1$ and reaches the lowest DPD/EOD of \emph{any} method on
Bail ($0.001$) but at a real utility cost ($\mathrm{AUC}{=}0.61$, more than
$0.35$ below the non-fair backbones)---an aggressive point on the
fairness--utility frontier rather than a free lunch, and a useful illustration
of why TrustFedGNN's \emph{constrained} formulation (BFWA's hard budget $\tau$,
Section~\ref{sec:bfwa}) is preferable to an unconstrained auto-tuned penalty:
the constraint lets us fix how much fairness we buy, rather than letting a
controller find out by overshooting.

The contrast with DP-FedAvg is especially telling. At the same
$\epsilon$, the only other private method in the table collapses in utility
(Section~\ref{sec:exp-privacy}), so ``adding privacy'' the standard way is not a
viable option for these models---making TrustFedGNN's near-free privacy a
qualitative, not merely quantitative, advantage. Finally, on Elliptic---a
$200$k-node cryptocurrency graph beyond the reach of every centralised fairness
baseline---TrustFedGNN attains the best subgroup fairness at detection accuracy
comparable to the non-fair backbone, demonstrating that trustworthy federated
fraud detection scales to real, industrial-size graphs.

\subsection{Privacy--utility: FTGD vs.\ full-gradient DP}
\label{sec:exp-privacy}
Figure~\ref{fig:privacy} sweeps the privacy budget $\epsilon$. FTGD retains
essentially full utility and fairness across $\epsilon\in[0.5,16]$, because it
privatises only the two-dimensional fairness statistic. In contrast, DP-FedAvg's
isotropic gradient noise collapses AUC toward chance at small $\epsilon$. This is
the central privacy result: \emph{TrustFedGNN provides sensitive-attribute
differential privacy at negligible cost}, where the standard mechanism is
unusable on compact GNNs.

\subsection{Robustness to Byzantine and fairness-poisoning attacks}
\label{sec:exp-robust}
Table~\ref{tab:robustness} reports utility retained under Gaussian, ALIE, and
fairness-poisoning attacks with $2/10$ malicious clients, across aggregators;
Figure~\ref{fig:robust} sweeps the number of Byzantine clients. FedAvg and plain
BFWA collapse under attack, and---critically---plain BFWA is \emph{captured} by
the fairness-poisoning attack that fabricates fairness statistics.
TrustFedGNN-Robust (robust\_bfwa) preserves utility and fairness by screening
malicious updates before applying the fairness constraint, matching or exceeding
dedicated robust aggregators while remaining fair.

\subsection{Scalability and sustainability}
Table~\ref{tab:efficiency} also reports communication and efficiency. TrustFedGNN
transmits only model weights, so its per-round communication equals FedAvg's;
fairness and privacy therefore incur \emph{no} communication overhead. The
method scales to $K{=}20$ clients and to the $200$k-node Elliptic graph on
commodity CPU hardware.

\paragraph{Scaling to millions of nodes.}
\label{sec:scaling}
To verify that the framework is not confined to graphs that fit in memory, we
additionally run it on \textbf{ogbn-products} \cite{hu2020ogb}---an Amazon
co-purchase graph of $2.4$M nodes and $123$M (symmetrised) edges, an order of
magnitude larger than Elliptic and far beyond full-batch training.
TrustFedGNN's three components are all local or $O(K)$ operations, so they
compose directly with standard mini-batch neighbor sampling
\cite{hamilton2017graphsage}: each client trains on sampled subgraphs, FSER
reweights the sampled edges, FTGD privatises the per-batch group statistics,
and BFWA is unchanged. As on Elliptic, ogbn-products carries no protected
demographic attribute, so both its binary target and its sensitive attribute
are documented structural proxies (Appendix~\ref{app:data}): we therefore
report these numbers as evidence that the method \emph{trains and behaves
sensibly at scale}, not as a demographic-fairness claim---that claim rests on
the five real datasets above.

Table~\ref{tab:large-scale} reports all six methods trained end-to-end on the
full $2.4$M-node graph, six clients' worth of neighbor-sampled mini-batches,
for $15$ communication rounds on commodity CPU hardware (a single
container, no GPU). Three findings stand out. First, utility is genuinely
learnable at this scale: FedAvg-GAT, FairSIN, and both TrustFedGNN variants all
converge to AUC $0.86$--$0.89$, confirming that FSER message passing, FTGD
statistic privatisation, and BFWA aggregation compose correctly with
mini-batch sampling rather than degrading into noise. Second,
\textbf{DP-FedAvg's utility collapses to AUC $0.556$}---far below its
performance on the five smaller datasets (Table~\ref{tab:main-auc})---showing
that naïve per-parameter DP noise, tolerable on models with a few thousand
parameters, becomes actively harmful once mini-batch gradient variance is
already high; this is exactly the failure mode FTGD's 2D statistic
privatisation (Section~\ref{sec:ftgd}) is designed to avoid, and TrustFedGNN's
own DP variant confirms it: it loses only $0.001$ AUC ($0.862$ vs.\ $0.861$
without DP) relative to its non-private counterpart at $\varepsilon\approx 8.0$,
versus DP-FedAvg's $0.32$ absolute-AUC collapse. Third,
\textbf{FaVGNN's adversarial training diverges under sampling at this scale}:
after four healthy rounds (AUC rising to $0.83$) its discriminator overwhelms
the encoder and the classifier collapses to a near-constant prediction (final
AUC $0.495$, chance level), with the corresponding DPD/EOD of $0.000$ reported
in Table~\ref{tab:large-scale} being a spurious side effect of that collapse,
not a fairness achievement---we flag this explicitly ($^{\dagger}$) rather
than let a broken run masquerade as a fair one. This is a genuine
generalisation limitation of adversarial debiasing under the noisier,
smaller-batch gradients that mini-batch sampling induces on million-node
graphs, and (to our knowledge) has not been previously reported for FaVGNN;
we view stabilising adversarial FL objectives under sampling as useful future
work rather than a flaw specific to our comparison. TrustFedGNN's per-round
wall-clock is $2.4$--$2.5\times$ FedAvg's (Table~\ref{tab:large-scale},
last column) on this CPU configuration, the price of FSER's edge-reweighted
message passing and BFWA's Frank--Wolfe iterations at this scale; this
overhead is amortised and communication-free (Table~\ref{tab:efficiency}), and
would shrink substantially under GPU-batched sparse operations, which we
leave to future engineering work. Overall, this confirms that the method's
per-client and $O(K)$ server operations impose no barrier to industrial-scale
deployment, while also surfacing genuine scale-dependent failure modes
(naïve DP, adversarial instability) that a purely small-scale evaluation
would have missed.
